Bayesian learning and posterior prediction

Project 01. In my first assignment, a model of PGA scores predicted a mean of 71.22 strokes and a 30.11% chance that a round would exceed 75. I recovered both numbers using the same 7,577 rounds.

The high-score prediction is where the model struggles. Only 684 rounds (9.03%) exceeded 75. A model can get the average right and still give a poor estimate of an unusually high score. This comparison uses the rounds on which the model was fitted.

Monetary policy and recession risk

Project 02. My second assignment favored a Federal Reserve rule with the same response to positive and negative output gaps. The simpler rule received 95.06% posterior probability. I recovered 95.06% with the original prior. Widening that prior raised the weight to 96.44%.

In the recession exercise, I traced the 12.36% estimate labeled February 2027 to a February 2025 predictor row. Using February 2026 predictors, the portfolio equity-return definition and a dated training cutoff gave 8.64% for February 2027. That outcome is unobserved in the study. Across the twelve 2008 targets, the corrected forecasts had lower squared probability error and higher logarithmic loss than the saved coursework forecasts. All twelve months were in recession, and the original fit admitted labels recorded after the forecast dates.

Persistence and shock propagation

Project 03. In my third assignment, a one-unit shock grew to 1.20 one period later in a two-lag model. The response eventually faded, but the sum of its effects approached 10. I recovered the shock path and verified that total analytically.

In a separate experiment, I asked how often a nominal 95% interval for the average would contain its true value. With 200 persistent observations and 10,000 simulated samples, treating observations as independent gave 35.27% coverage. Using their known correlation gave 95.00%. The gain is in the reliability of uncertainty estimates under the simulated conditions.

Lag uncertainty and Bayesian model averaging

Project 04. In my fourth assignment, I compared eight ways to specify how much of a series’ history to use. The two-lag model received 71.54% of the posterior weight. I recovered that result and the estimated persistence of the eight-lag model, 0.8006 in class and 0.8008 in the portfolio.

What survives a change in prior beliefs? The weight on the two-lag model ranged from 60.01% to 80.96%, yet persistence averaged across the eight models moved only from 0.8430 to 0.8504. The strength of the preference for two lags is sensitive. The conclusion about persistence is much steadier.

U.S. GDP growth at risk

Project 05. In my fifth assignment, a downside scenario for U.S. GDP put the fifth percentile of growth from 2026Q1 to 2027Q1 at −0.218 log percentage points. My portfolio estimate was −0.342 with the same pandemic smoothing. The original chart paired forecasts for different horizons. I matched the horizons and compared both methods against realized annual growth.

Across 78 matched forecast dates, the portfolio model lowered root mean squared error from 2.19 to 2.07 log percentage points, a 5.6% reduction. It also lowered loss on the fifth percentile. Over the 13 post-pandemic dates, the coursework method had the smaller error, 0.58 versus 0.63, and the lower downside loss. The annual windows overlap and use revised GDP. The 2027Q1 forecast is outside this evaluation.

Forecasting U.S. payrolls

Project 06. In my doctoral time-series course, my model put the April 2026 U.S. payroll gain at 61,140 jobs. The first announcement was +115,000, a miss of 53,860. That result prompted two questions. Which indicators improve accuracy over many months? How much does the treatment of the pandemic change the answer?

Across 216 monthly releases from 2007 through 2024, adding industrial production and CPI to employment history reduced root mean squared error by 24.7%. Adding equities and the Treasury spread reduced it a further 0.14%. All three models missed the four first announcements from March through June 2020 with their 90% intervals.

The original estimate came closer in April than either five-variable refit. Re-estimating with the same smoothing rule gave +46,436 jobs, while retaining the pandemic observations gave −141,062. In a separate comparison using identical data and model settings, smoothing cut average absolute error from 226,000 to 146,000 jobs over 34 post-pandemic releases. All 34 were gains. The historical evaluation aligns publication dates and uses revised inputs.

Payroll forecasts after a shock

Project 07. In my doctoral time-series course I estimated that U.S. payrolls would rise by about 289,000 jobs from March to September 2026. The September release showed a six-month gain of 394,000. My model retained the pandemic observations and estimated that shocks in March 2020 had about 104 times their usual variance. The assignment left a question open. Would smoothing those observations have produced a better forecast at the same six-month horizon?

Across 28 post-pandemic forecast dates, the model with temporary volatility cut root mean squared error from 611,000 to 473,000 jobs on revised employment data. Smoothing the training history and starting each forecast with the latest observed conditions did slightly better at 459,000. All 28 periods ended in gains.

The answer changed for someone forecasting the sum of the initial monthly jobs announcements. Temporary volatility then had the lowest error of the three statistical models at 665,000 jobs. A simple rule that repeated the previous six-month gain did better still at 515,000.
