Monetary policy and recession risk

Project 02. Do policy responses to economic slack require separate slopes, and what do financial indicators reveal about recession risk twelve months ahead?

The policy analysis uses 239 U.S. quarters, 1955Q1–2014Q3. The symmetric rule receives 96.4% posterior model probability within the two specified alternatives under the primary prior. The long-run inflation response has posterior median 1.60 and a central 90% interval of [0.95, 2.48]. Residual dependence limits the policy interpretation.

The recession application makes forecast origins, target dates, and label availability explicit. In its twelve observed 2008 targets, Brier loss improves relative to a matched frequency benchmark while negative log score worsens. This revised-data, pseudo-real-time exercise does not establish general forecasting superiority.

The Python implementation separates data contracts, priors, models, Gibbs samplers, posterior results, and forecast evaluation. Independent numerical integration checks model evidence; scientific controls run in continuous integration.

Persistence and shock propagation

Project 03. How can models with the same long-run shock total produce different transmission paths, and how does persistence change the precision of an average?

Three stable autoregressive specifications share an infinite accumulated response of ten to a one-unit innovation, but their responses differ in timing and variance. One stable AR(2) initially amplifies the innovation by 20% before decaying. Root analysis separates stable, unstable, and unit-circle cases; a finite moving-average control distinguishes stationarity from invertibility.

In the designed stationary AR(1), 200 observations have the mean-estimation precision of 11.05 independent observations. Across 10,000 replications, a nominal 95% interval that assumes independence attains only 35.27% coverage. These are controlled known-parameter simulation results, not estimates from an observed economic dataset or posterior credible intervals.

Responses are checked through companion matrices, recursion, controlled shock paths, and an independent linear filter. The object-oriented package records seeds, initial conditions, burn-in, numerical errors, and artifact checksums.

Lag uncertainty and Bayesian model averaging

Project 04. How much does the lag-order decision affect what we learn about persistence and shock transmission?

The study compares AR(1)-AR(8) on 192 identical targets from a supplied 200-observation series. Under the primary prior, AR(2) receives 71.54% posterior model probability, while other orders retain meaningful weight. The model-averaged coefficient-sum mean is 0.8478, with a central 90% interval of [0.7901, 0.9029]; fixed AR(8) gives 0.8008, with interval [0.7276, 0.8739].

Between-model uncertainty contributes 10.38% of the primary coefficient-sum variance. Across coefficient-prior sensitivities, AR(2)’s weight ranges from 60.01% to 80.96%, while model-averaged means remain between 0.8430 and 0.8504. Finite shock responses preserve parameter and model uncertainty, with pointwise intervals and explicit root-stability checks.

All 24 main fits have an independent retained-array replay in the recorded environment. Chib evidence is checked against numerical integration, including scalar tail calculations that distinguish zero sampled events from zero probability. The series’ generating process and economic identity remain unverified; the findings are conditional inference rather than forecast-performance or causal claims.

U.S. GDP growth at risk

Project 05. How well do GDP forecasts describe the downside, especially after a contraction?

Across 86 forecasts of four-quarter U.S. growth, a random walk with drift had the lowest error for average growth. A trend AR(4) delivered a better downside forecast when scored on the fifth percentile. Its quantile loss averaged 0.419, compared with 0.457 for drift. Growth still fell below its downside threshold eight times in 86 forecasts, concentrated in the financial crisis and the pandemic.

One forecast explains 57% of the AR’s total squared annual error. After the contraction in 2020Q2, the model projected another 11.50% decline over the following year. GDP grew 11.68% in log terms. The study uses historical GDP release dates and revised observations, and tests how retaining those pandemic quarters affects subsequent risk estimates.

Forecasting U.S. payrolls

Project 06. What does a labor-market analyst gain from adding production, prices and financial markets to employment history?

Across 216 monthly U.S. payroll releases from 2007 through 2024, adding industrial production and CPI reduced root mean squared error by 24.7%. Adding the S&P 500 and the Treasury spread reduced it a further 0.14%. The financial indicators helped more with the probability of a job loss. The Brier score improved from 0.150 to 0.141.

The five-variable model’s 90% interval covered 96.3% of first announcements, yet all three models missed every release from March through June 2020. Those four months accounted for 91.2% of the five-variable model’s total squared error. The study uses historical publication dates and revised inputs, scores both first and revised payroll estimates, and examines how the pandemic history changes the risk assigned to April 2026.
