The research model

When some directions are more likely

I ran the same two-draw comparison for a von Mises target centered at zero. Here the distribution has a preferred direction. The sine records which side of that direction a point falls on. The cosine measures its alignment and takes the same value on either side at equal angular distance.

On the very same pairs of angles, the sine average had about 36% less variance than an independent pair. The cosine average had about 7% more.

Derive the variance ratio

For the uniform target, the potential is constant. With kinetic energy K(p) = |p|, the momentum stays fixed throughout each flight. A fresh symmetric Laplace momentum sets the direction, and the angular speed is one. Write δ for the flight angle in radians.

Start with θ uniform on the circle. Choose a sign independently with equal probability and set θ′ = θ ± δ, modulo 2π. Both endpoints are uniform. For any positive integer k, f(θ) = cos(kθ) has mean zero and variance 1/2. Averaging over the two directions gives

E[f(θ′) | θ] = cos(kδ) f(θ).

Consequently the correlation of successive readings is cos(kδ). Dividing the variance of their average by the variance of the average of two independent readings gives

variance ratio = 1 + cos(kδ).

At δ = 150° = 5π/6, the ratios are 1 − √3/2 and 3/2 for the first and second harmonics. These are exact two-draw calculations. An estimate formed from a long chain also depends on correlations at longer lags.

From this circle to my research

In my dissertation, I study Ari Pakman’s exact HMC sampler with Laplace momentum on the circle. Between turns the angle travels at constant speed. A sufficiently energetic trajectory can go all the way around the circle, while one with less energy can reach a turning point and reverse.

That geometry leads to the question behind this illustration. For a particular quantity I want to estimate, when do successive readings offset each other enough to reduce the long-run variance of their average?
